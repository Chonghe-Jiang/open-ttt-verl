Test-time training

Learning to evolve algorithms in thought space

We separate deciding what to try from deciding how to implement it. A small guidance policy writes the next proposal; a large frozen executor implements it as a complete program; every program receives one exact score. Test-time reinforcement learning then updates only the small policy. One run takes a Polyomino Packing solver from 27.81 to 91.89 — above the reported multi-agent and human references on the same evaluator.

Method

Two models, two spaces

The guidance policy reads a compact summary of the current solver and proposes how it should change — a new mechanism, representation, or search strategy. The executor receives that proposal together with the complete parent program, implements the change, and writes a summary of what actually changed. Proposal and summary form the evolving state in thought space; the complete program stays lossless in execution space, so a lossy summary can never silently delete a working mechanism.

Over a run, the guidance policy learns which kinds of changes tend to pay off: it treats each summary as a state, proposes a transition, and is updated by the score that comes back. Progress accumulates only when all three links hold — the proposal actually changes behavior, the implementation preserves what the parent already does well, and the summary is clear enough to serve as the next state.

Results

A single run, from 27.81 to 91.89

Polyomino Packing (FrontierCS problem 0) asks a single program to pack irregular pieces into a minimal rectangle across 70 hidden cases. Every proposal must become one complete solver; every solver receives an exact score. The best Guidance-TTT solver — 2,759 lines of C++, found at step 27 — scores 91.89, above the reported CORAL four-agent system and the released human reference on the same evaluator.

  • Steps 0–3Discover the topology. Row packing becomes symmetry-aware skyline search with annealing — 27.81 → 87.56.
  • Steps 4–12Adapt the search. Width priors, conflict prediction, and multi-modal restarts — to 90.84.
  • Steps 13–20Close the feedback loop. Aspect, density, and fragmentation become live control signals — to 91.76.
  • Steps 21–27Predict residual space. Gap depth becomes shape compatibility, then multi-step look-ahead — to 91.89.
  • Steps 28–31Saturate. 445 more valid programs, no further gain.

The kind of improvement shifts over the run: first a new representation, then live feedback signals, then a mechanism that exploits them. The final improvement, at step 27, came from a one-sentence proposal — "simulate placing multiple pieces sequentially in predicted optimal positions" — implemented as a cheap three-step residual-rectangle forecast inside the placement score.

  1. Separate guidance helps before any learning.

    Under GPT-OSS-120B, adding frozen 8B guidance moves 69.92 to 80.87. Under GPT-5.4-low, it reaches 77.16 versus 69.39 for direct evolution. Under GLM-5.2, it reaches 84.85 versus 78.36 for direct OpenEvolve. Splitting proposal from implementation helps even while the guidance weights stay fixed.

  2. Training the policy adds a further gain.

    With GLM-5.2 execution and the same 3,840-candidate budget, frozen guidance reaches 84.85 and trained guidance 91.89. The budget is matched, but this is one run of each; repeated seeds are needed before the gap can be read as a causal effect.

  3. Search procedure matters alongside executor capability.

    Under GPT-OSS-120B, the one-agent CORAL snapshot reaches 45.23 after 54 evaluations, direct mutation reaches 69.92, and frozen guidance reaches 80.87. These runs are not budget-matched, but they show that model scale alone does not determine search quality.

  4. Guidance-model scale matters less.

    Replacing the 8B policy with a 27B one changes nothing (80.18 vs 80.20 under GPT-OSS-120B), and neither does a longer reasoning window (32K vs 48K context). Once the policy can name the useful mechanisms, the bottleneck is execution.

What 91.89 does not prove. The matched GLM-5.2 comparison separates frozen guidance (84.85) from trained guidance (91.89) at the same sample count. It remains a single-run comparison: repeated seeds are needed to distinguish a stable learning effect from trajectory-level search variance, and the 500-iteration OpenEvolve run is not budget-matched.
Another result

From a scratch kernel to a hybrid GPU pipeline

We also apply Guidance-TTT to TriMul, the outgoing AlphaFold3 Triangle Multiplicative Update kernel from the TTT-Discover benchmark. Starting from a correct but slow GLM-5.2 implementation, the search discovers a kernel with a best observed latency of 1225.58 µs, an 8.30× improvement over its 10177.40 µs scratch seed. Lower is better throughout this section.

The training recipe is the same as in Polyomino: Qwen3-8B LoRA guidance, a frozen GLM-5.2 executor, the same summary-to-guidance communication and exact code for the executor, and the same adaptive entropic update over eight parents with sixteen rollouts each. We run this configuration for 30 updates, evaluating 3,840 candidates in total.

Evaluator version. TriMul uses the TTT-Discover evaluation contract with 18 correctness cases and seven timed leaderboard shapes, pinned to CUDA 12.8, PyTorch 2.7.1, and Triton 3.3.1. Where the efficiency comes from

Front end Fuse the bandwidth-bound work

One Triton kernel combines input normalization, five projections, sigmoid gates, masking, and the direct contraction layout.

Core Keep the regular N³ operation dense

torch.matmul delegates the batched contraction to cuBLAS and its mature Tensor Core path.

Back end Fuse the output path

A second Triton kernel reads the contraction layout directly and combines normalization, gating, and final projection.

The key result is not a single monolithic Triton kernel. It is a division of labor: Triton removes memory traffic and launch overhead around the contraction, while cuBLAS handles the regular compute-bound core. Direct [B,H,N,N] intermediates avoid explicit permutations; FP32 is retained for sensitive reductions and FP16 is used where Tensor Core throughput matters.

Optimization

A max-seeking update over proposals

Execution and scoring are expensive; the learned object is a small policy over proposals. Each update selects eight parent states from the history by PUCT, samples sixteen proposals per state, executes and scores all 128 candidates, and applies a critic-free REINFORCE step to proposal tokens only.

xi = exp[β(ri − rmax)] → Ai = xi / meanj≠i(xj) − 1 → L = −mean(ρi,t Ai log πθ)

Average-reward objectives underweight the event that matters most in discovery: a single candidate beating the current best. The entropic advantage re-weights each group exponentially, with β chosen per group so that DKL(qβ ‖ Uniform) = log 2 — flat groups get β = 0 and exert no pressure. Leave-one-out baselines prevent a strong sample from softening its own comparison; importance ratios ρ and a KL penalty to the reference policy keep off-policy credit usable and drift bounded.

Limitations

Why the search digs in one direction

The plateau after step 27 is not a failure of execution — post-best step means stay between 88.26 and 90.80. It is an on-policy feedback loop: a high score promotes a lineage into the history, PUCT concentrates rollouts on its descendants, and the policy is updated on that increasingly local distribution. Each successful local update makes a jump to a different family of algorithms less likely. The policy doesn't just learn which action worked; it reshapes the future data it will learn from.

A genuinely different lineage may need to begin by breaking something that works — accepting a temporary score debt while a new representation assembles its own mechanisms. That points at agentic test-time training: a system that learns not only the next research move but when to stop exploiting, launch a speculative branch, protect it through early losses, and judge it on multi-step return. The learned object is then not a single proposal but a policy over multi-step research programs.
